# The modulus stops being a constant, and its default is 128

**Date:** 2026-08-29 · **Status:** to test · **Branch:** `perf/modulus-128`

Every figure here comes from `backend/eval/modulus-cost.json`, written by
`backend/eval/modulus_cost.py` on 2026-08-29, or from `backend/eval/lock-budget.json` and
`backend/eval/partition-shape.json`. Nothing is quoted that was not measured, and the two
figures that are *derived* — the lock count `9P + 9` and the migration floor `4 x MODULUS` —
say so where they appear.

## What this responds to

Migration 0026 hard-coded `MODULUS = 256`. `partition-shape.json` recommended that number
when the binding constraint was the shared lock table, and it recommended it as "as many as
we can safely afford". `chore/partition-lock-budget` then sized `max_locks_per_transaction`
at 2,560 and the constraint went away. `modulus-cost.json` costed the modulus with nothing
stopping it, over seven rungs and two synthetic tenant populations on this installation's
real 13,549 passages.

The finding: **the benefit of a larger modulus is bounded and runs out early; the cost is
not bounded and grows faster than the modulus does.**

## The two curves

A tenant's query reaches `s + (1 - s) / P` of the corpus — `s` its own share, `P` the
modulus — so the whole benefit any modulus can offer is the `(1 - s) / P` term, which halves
per doubling. Planning roughly triples per doubling, measured across five of them.

Largest tenant's share of the corpus reached, 200 tenants under Zipf 1.0, mean of forty
draws, against a request's planning at the same rung:

| MODULUS | share reached | planning, post-stage-02 lexical | planning, `tsvector` as installed | locks/request |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32 | 0.1966 | 2.88 ms | 5.03 ms | 297 |
| 64 | 0.1803 | 5.83 ms | 12.69 ms | 585 |
| **128** | **0.1745** | **17.16 ms** | **47.86 ms** | **1,161** |
| 256 | 0.1746 | 37.39 ms | 143.09 ms | 2,313 |
| 512 | 0.1722 | 130.35 ms | 551.75 ms | 4,617 |

The tenant owns 0.1701 and cannot go below it. **128 and 256 are the same number in the
column that matters** — 0.1745 against 0.1746, which is inside the sampling noise of forty
draws — and 256 costs 2.2x the planning and exactly twice the locks. MODULUS 1024 was not
reachable at all: four attempts, four failures, the OOM killer terminating the backend during
planning and PostgreSQL going into crash recovery, with one attempt exceeding a 300-second
statement timeout planning a single statement.

## What shipped

**1. The default is 128, and 0026 no longer names a number.** `MODULUS =
settings.partition_modulus`, read once, at the moment the partitions are created. Every
later reader counts partitions out of the catalogue instead: the drift test, the RLS guard,
both diagnostics.

**2. `ZENITH_PARTITION_MODULUS`, wired the way `max_locks_per_transaction` was.** An
environment variable with a documented default — in `.env.example`, cross-referenced from
`docker/docker-compose.yml` beside the lock setting it has to move with — and a runtime check
that recomputes the answer off the running installation rather than trusting the file. The
rule an operator uses:

```
P  >=  (1 - s) / (epsilon * s)
```

holds a tenant of share `s` to `epsilon` extra rows. **128 is the default because it covers
the wide case, not because it is right for any particular installation.** For a 200-tenant
Zipf population (`s ~ 0.17`) it is 128; for this one (`s = 0.6106`) it is 13.

**3. `MIN_LOCKS_PER_TRANSACTION` becomes `4 x MODULUS`.** That is where the 1,024 it shipped
with came from — chosen at 256 to buy 43 concurrent searches — and locks are exactly linear
in the modulus: `lock-budget.json` measures 9.00 locks per partition-pair against the
schema's nine relations, and `modulus-cost.json` records `locks_for_request` as exactly
`9P + 9` at all five partitioned rungs. A constant would have halved the margin the first
time somebody halved the modulus.

**4. `zenith diagnose` evaluates the rule against the running installation.** Largest
tenant's share out of `pg_stats`, corpus size out of `reltuples`, modulus out of `pg_class`
— catalogue only, so it costs the same at 13,549 passages and at 322.6M. Before 0026 it names
the modulus to set; after 0026 it says whether the installed one fits. Never a failure: too
small a modulus is latency and not a leak.

`max_locks_per_transaction` stays at 2,560 although the default modulus is now half what it
was sized for. 10 x 256 covers 10 x 128 twice over, so an installation that raises the
modulus back needs no second change and no second restart.

And on a restore of the same corpus with 0026 applied at the default:

```
    of 256000 slots, room for 220 concurrent
OK  partition modulus: chunks: 128 partitions, largest tenant owns 0.611 of ~13,549 rows,
    so the rule wants 13 at 5%, and it reads 0.5% more than it owns
```

`pg_stats` reports 0.61059856 for the larger tenant; the exact count is 8,273 of 13,549,
which is 0.6105985. The estimate route is not an approximation of the datum
`partition-shape.json` quotes — it is where that datum came from.

## Two things an operator has to know

- **The running installation cannot apply 0026 today, at any modulus.** Its Postgres is still
  at `max_locks_per_transaction = 64`; the compose file's 2,560 reaches a container only when
  the container is **recreated**, not restarted. 0026's preflight refuses below 512 at the
  default modulus, which is the correct outcome and not a regression: at 64 the *downgrade*
  is what fails, halfway, which is the state `CONTRIBUTING.md` requires a working rollback to
  avoid.
- **0026 does not `ANALYZE` what it builds.** Four of 256 partitions had a row estimate at
  the moment the migration returned; autovacuum reaches the rest over the following minutes.
  Until then the modulus check declines to answer rather than sizing against 2% of the
  corpus. Running `ANALYZE chunks` after a cutover is worth doing for the planner's sake as
  much as for this check's, and whether 0026 should do it itself is a question for whoever
  owns that migration.

sharing.

## Still open

- **Whether 128 is the right default, or 64 is.** Argued in the branch's report rather than
  silently: the benefit of 128 over 64 for the wide case is 0.6% of the corpus, and the cost
  is 11.3 ms of planning per search on the post-stage-02 path and 35 ms on the engine
  installed today. The counter-argument is that 64 is the `epsilon = 10%` answer where 128 is
  the 5% one, and that the parameter now makes the default the thing an operator overrides
  rather than the thing they live with. Left at 128 because that is what the document's own
  tables recommend at the tighter target.
- **Whether 0026 should `ANALYZE`.** See above. Not taken here: it belongs to the migration's
  owner and it has a cost at 322.6M passages that nothing on disk has measured.
